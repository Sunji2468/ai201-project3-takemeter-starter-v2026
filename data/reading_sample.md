## 9. Ask HN: What is holding back robots from being deployed everywhere?

Source: https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50467024

I hope this is not a bad analogy but here goes.

Have you seen a Costco Food Court? The first time I saw it I thought of how inefficient it was. All these people crowded around a pickup window waiting for their number to be called.

They could easily build some sort of notification system so people would get a message on their phone when their order was ready. They could be shopping around the store rather than all waiting here.

Such an obvious opportunity for automation.

But after watching how they actually work and reflecting on it for a bit, I started to see why they don't do that.

- Letting people walk away likely means more orders will be piling up in their limited working space waiting to be picked up.

- These workers were fast. Juggling multiple orders at the same time. The "state" of the order is all in one place, a physical receipt and that state doesn't really change. Adding software to their workflow would almost certainly slow it all down (for reference, see hospital software).

- You'd have to train people on how to use the notification system. What happens when the system changes? What about security? What if someone were to hijack it and start sending out fake notifications?

My takeaway here is that every decision has trade-offs so before you think something can be automated you should understand what the manual process is first and why it works the way it does.

## 14. Ask HN: How do you evaluate the skills your AI agents use?

Source: https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50427183

There are two ways I see to do this:

1. Tell your agent to eval the skill itself (https://code.claude.com/docs/en/plugin-evals (https://code.claude.com/docs/en/plugin-evals)). This is easier and faster, but less accurate, and harder to extend to capture things like code quality.

2. Build your own benchmark out of work your normally do, and then eval the skill on that dataset. This is harder and more annoying (because creating benchmarks is hard) but provides deeper insight into how the skill affects the agent in known scenarios.

It also depends on what the skill is doing. If it's creating an artifact (like a report, easier to eval) then your strategy is very different than if the skill is changing agent behavior (like telling it to use cheap subagents under XYZ conditions, harder to eval).

The importance of doing this grows with the number of people using the skill. Working on my own projects, I don't usually bother. Working in an enterprise monorepo, suddenly the risk of shipping a bad skill is amplified, and evals become much more attractive.

Also note that skill behavior changes across different models. A skill built for Opus 4.8 may not be necessary on Opus 5.5. A skill built for Opus 5.5 might cause Haiku 5.5 to behave erratically.

TLDR: tell your agent to do it, and think about building your own.

## 25. Ask HN: Do you still enjoy coding in C++?

I'm really torn about C++. I love C++ but there are things that I really don't like about it as well. It's a fantastic language in a lot of ways, but it carries around 40+ years of cruft, of things that you wouldn't do like that if you were inventing a programming language today.

I haven't written C++ professionally for many years at this point, and my memories of using it professionally (pre C++ 11) are not happy ones: having to agree on a strict subset of the language that we were allowed to use, which avoided features that any of the compilers didn't support well, or that my teammates didn't like.
